          Abstract. Deep learning has improved breast cancer detection in
          mammograms, but most models are trained on patients without im-
          plants. In implant patients, the implant obscures parenchyma and masks
          lesions such as masses and calcifications, lowering sensitivity; because
          relevant information is partially missing, prior models can still produce
          confident yet unreliable predictions. To address this, we propose an
          evidential detection network that couples a mammography foundation
          model backbone with a prototype-based Dempster–Shafer network,
          pairing each prediction with an explicit uncertainty estimate and a
          prototype-based explanation. Among four SOTA baseline models, our
          model achieved the highest AUROC (0.864), balanced accuracy (0.828),
          and specificity (0.793) in the breast implant dataset EMBED, improving
          over its Mammo-CLIP backbone (AUROC 0.781). The higher balanced
          accuracy came mainly from improved specificity, with sensitivity remain-
          ing competitive. For correctly detected cancers, the model grounded
          each prediction in clinically meaningful prototypes that share lesion
          descriptors with the target image and assigned them low uncertainty,
          supporting interpretable and confident decisions

          Keywords: Uncertainty · Breast cancer · Implants · Evidential learning


1     Introduction
Breast cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer among women, with an
estimated 2.3 million new cases and 670,000 deaths worldwide in 2022 [1]. Be-
cause early detection substantially improves survival, mammography serves as
the gold standard for early screening, providing high-resolution visualization of
lesions such as microcalcifications and masses. However, a growing number of
women have breast implants from augmentation or reconstruction, and these
implants complicate screening. As shown in Figure 1, the implant appears as a
large high-density region that blocks the field of view and compresses the breast
tissue. This occlusion makes lesions harder to detect and reduces the sensitivity
of mammography in patients with implants.

    To improve screening accuracy, deep learning has been widely applied to
breast cancer detection. Early methods relied on convolutional neural networks
(CNNs), such as the model of Wu et al. [14], which achieved promising results.
More recently, multimodal vision-language models [11, 5, 8, 7] have incorporated
textual information from diagnostic reports and achieved strong screening per-
formance. These models are typically trained and evaluated on mammograms
without implants and their reliability on implant cases remains largely unveri-
fied. When an implant occludes the breast, it systematically blocks critical tissue
information and leaves the model in a state of genuine ignorance rather than
mere ambiguity. A standard confidence score cannot distinguish this missing-
information case from ordinary uncertainty, so the model may still output a
confident but unreliable prediction. This limitation motivates the use of evi-
dence theory. Whereas Bayesian approximations [6] and deep ensembles [10]
output only a scalar confidence, the Dempster-Shafer theory [12, 3] represents
belief and ignorance as separate quantities. This explicit treatment of ignorance
is exactly what implant screening requires, because it lets the model flag cases
where missing information prevents a reliable decision instead of issuing a con-
fident misprediction.
    To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to combine uncertainty
modeling with breast cancer detection in patients with implants. To close this
gap, we propose an evidential network that couples a vision-language foundation
backbone with a prototype-based Dempster-Shafer module (Figure 2).

2.2   Prototype-DST Module for Uncertainty Modeling

The Dempster–Shafer theory of evidence (DST) [12, 3] explicitly mod-
els uncertainty by distributing belief mass over the frame of discernment
Ω = {ω1 , . . . , ωK } (K = 2 for our binary task). Given the bag-level representa-
tion z of a mammogram, we follow the evidential neural network (ENN) [4] to
map z to mass functions through class-specific prototypes. We use J prototypes
per class, {pk,j : k = 1, . . . , K, j = 1, . . . , J}, where each prototype pk,j
is assigned to class ωk , initialized by K-means clustering of the training-set
embeddings, and refined as a parameter during training. Each prototype acts
as a piece of evidence whose strength decreases with the Euclidean distance

3     Experimental Results
3.1   Experimental Settings
Our experiments were conducted on the Emory Breast Imaging Dataset (EM-
BED)[9], using only images from patients with breast implants. This subset com-
prises 42,857 images from 4,235 patients, split into 34,448 training and 8,409 test
images. We trained the model using five-fold cross-validation, setting 10 proto-
types per class (20 in total for this binary task). The regularization weights λ1 ,
λ2 and λ3 were all set to 0.1. Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 5 × 10−3
was used with 250 training epochs.

3.2   Results and Discussion
To evaluate our model, we compared it with four recent breast cancer detec-
tion models that have shown strong performance, namely MIL[11], GLAM[5],
Mammo-CLIP[8], and Mammo-FM[7]. Table 1 presents the results. Our model
attained the highest AUROC (0.864), balanced accuracy (bACC,
0.828), and specificity (0.793). Our sensitivity (0.853) remained competitive

    Beyond producing a prediction score, the prototype-DST module offers two
further properties, namely interpretability and uncertainty quantification. Fig-
ure 3 illustrates both for two true-positive cases. Each test mammogram is
matched to its most similar learned prototype, and the two images share the
same lesion descriptors, which makes the basis of the prediction explicit. The
matched prototypes and low uncertainty together yield interpretable,
confident decisions.
    This study has limitations. First, external validation across diverse institu-
tions and scanners is needed to confirm generalizability. Second, quantitative cal-
ibration of the uncertainty estimates would strengthen their reliability. Third,
comparison with radiologist performance and extension beyond binary cancer
labels in implant cases are warranted. These will be addressed in future work.


4     Conclusion
We propose an evidential neural network based on a vision-language model for
uncertainty quantification in patients with breast implants. It couples a Mammo-
CLIP image encoder with a prototype-DST module that computes mass func-
tions to yield prediction scores and uncertainty estimates. The model achieved an
AUROC of 0.864, outperforming the strongest vision-language baseline (0.813),
while providing interpretable prototype evidence and low uncertainty for confi-
